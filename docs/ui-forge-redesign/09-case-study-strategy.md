# Portfolio and case-study strategy

## Strategic role

For UI Forge, the portfolio is not a gallery. It is the primary proof that one founder-led practice can understand a business problem, make strong design decisions, implement them responsibly, and leave the client with a maintainable result.

The current portfolio has one substantial internal case study—the UI Forge website itself. That is useful evidence of taste and implementation, but it cannot independently prove client collaboration, external constraints, commercial results, or breadth. The redesign should treat content acquisition as a prerequisite, not solve the gap with fictional cards or vague visuals.

## Evidence standard

Every public project should record:

- client/project name and permission status;
- industry and relevant context;
- dates or time period;
- UI Forge's exact role and collaborators;
- services delivered and important exclusions;
- original problem, constraints, and success definition;
- visual/technical artefacts with captions;
- platform/stack only where relevant to the decision;
- outcome type: measured, client-reported, qualitative, proxy, or unavailable;
- testimonial source and publication permission;
- image rights and any confidentiality limits.

Use labels such as “Measured outcome”, “Client-reported”, or “Project objective” where a casual reader could otherwise mistake a goal for a result.

## Work listing page

### Structure

1. Short premise explaining what the collection demonstrates.
2. One flagship project with a substantial visual and concise problem/outcome story.
3. Remaining selected projects in a varied but consistent editorial list.
4. Capability/evidence legend if role boundaries need explanation.
5. Compact working-principles block.
6. Project-fit CTA.

Do not introduce filters with fewer than six materially different projects. When inventory eventually justifies them, filter by buyer-relevant need—website/commerce, digital product, ongoing evolution—not by visual style or every technology.

### Project card treatment

Each card should contain:

- one dominant, art-directed image or short user-controlled clip;
- project/client name;
- one-sentence problem or change;
- UI Forge role and project type;
- one outcome/evidence line where available;
- a clear case-study link.

The entire visual/title region may be clickable, but text links and focus states must remain explicit. Avoid equal-sized template cards if one project deserves a different scale. Avoid tiny device mockups, hover-only metadata, autoplay video, and decorative “View case” cursor effects.

## Case-study information architecture

### 1. Case-study hero

- Project/client and concise change statement.
- Hero visual that demonstrates the product rather than merely placing it in a device.
- Fact block: industry, role, services, platform where relevant, timeframe, launch status.
- Outcome/evidence cue, clearly qualified.
- Optional anchor navigation for long studies.

### 2. Client context

Explain the organisation, audience, business moment, and why the project mattered. Give enough context to understand decisions without reproducing a company profile.

### 3. Problem and constraints

State the original state, user/business problem, practical limitations, dependencies, and success measures. Include what was explicitly out of scope. This is where judgment becomes credible.

### 4. Approach and decision frame

Describe discovery, content/product definition, major alternatives, and why the chosen route fit. Surface platform trade-offs where they mattered. Avoid a generic five-step process repeated verbatim in every case.

### 5. Before and after

Show equivalent states whenever permitted: hierarchy, workflow, content model, responsive behaviour, speed, maintenance, or conversion path. Label images and make the comparison understandable without a draggable slider. A slider can be a progressive enhancement with buttons and accessible descriptions.

### 6. Visual design showcase

Use a sequence of high-resolution crops, complete screens, components, and states. Captions should identify the decision being shown: information priority, navigation, form behaviour, systemisation, accessibility, or brand expression. Do not rely on ornamental mockups as captions substitute.

### 7. Responsive experience

Demonstrate meaningful adaptations across viewport sizes—navigation model, project cards, tables, forms, density—not three device frames containing the same scaled page. Include touch and small-screen constraints where they affected design.

### 8. Development and technical detail

Explain the architecture only to the depth that proves the project's requirements were met. Possible material includes:

- framework/CMS/platform decision and trade-offs;
- component/content model;
- integrations and data flows;
- accessibility and performance work;
- forms, consent, analytics, or commerce considerations;
- migration, redirects, or SEO preservation;
- QA and deployment approach;
- maintainability and client editing model.

Technical diagrams should clarify a real relationship, not decorate the page.

### 9. Interactions

Use short, user-controlled clips or prototypes for behaviour that a static image cannot explain. Provide poster images, captions, controls, and reduced-motion alternatives. Never make interaction evidence depend on hover alone.

### 10. Results

Order results from strongest to weakest evidence:

1. measured business outcome;
2. measured user/technical outcome linked to the brief;
3. client-reported result;
4. observable delivery outcome;
5. qualitative project reflection;
6. stated objective when post-launch evidence is unavailable.

Include measurement window, baseline, source, and caveat. Do not turn Lighthouse scores into commercial impact or round qualitative feedback into percentages.

### 11. Testimonial

Use a project-specific quote with full name, role, company, permission, and relationship context. If a quote is not available, omit the module. Never create a generic anonymous endorsement to complete the layout.

### 12. Ownership and handover

Because ownership is a differentiator, document what the client received: accounts, source, documentation, training, hosting arrangement, maintenance responsibilities, and post-launch plan.

### 13. Related projects

Recommend one or two projects that answer a different evaluation question, not simply the next items in a carousel. If inventory is thin, link to Work and a relevant insight instead.

### 14. Final CTA

Use a problem-oriented invitation: discuss a similar challenge, bring an undecided platform question, or review an existing product. Repeat response expectations and link to Start a project.

## Project media system

For each case, capture a planned set rather than ad hoc screenshots:

- 16:10 or 3:2 lead composition;
- full desktop page or key flow;
- 4:5 editorial crop;
- 1:1 detail crop;
- mobile 9:16 or device-free screen sequence;
- interaction poster plus short clip where necessary;
- system/artefact image;
- before-state equivalent where allowed.

Define focal points and alternate crops in the content model. Prefer direct interface captures and contextual project assets to a repeated mockup generator. Optimise with `next/image`, appropriate `sizes`, and meaningful alt text; decorative composites should use empty alt.

## Content currently missing or insufficient

- Two to four permissioned external client projects.
- Named client context and role boundaries.
- Before-state evidence.
- Business or user outcome measurements with provenance.
- Project-specific testimonials.
- Founder/client/process photography.
- Technical and decision artefacts from real delivery.
- Content that demonstrates post-launch ownership and care.
- Enough projects to justify filtering or a broad service claim.
- A consistent evidence taxonomy and rights record.

The unpublished local article is not a substitute for work proof. Likewise, the studio's own website case should be explicit about being self-initiated.

## Minimum viable launch portfolio

A credible redesign launch should target:

- one detailed flagship external case;
- one additional external case showing a different problem type;
- the UI Forge site case, clearly identified as an internal brand/product case;
- one lighter project note only if its client, role, and evidence are still meaningful.

If only the internal case is available, delay the portfolio-led redesign or launch a more modest interim structure. Visual confidence cannot compensate for unsupported breadth.

## Content-production workflow

1. Inventory candidate projects, permissions, assets, and outcome evidence.
2. Score candidates for relevance, contrast, visual material, technical interest, and proof strength.
3. Interview client and project owner; capture the decision story before writing.
4. Build an evidence sheet and separate fact, client report, interpretation, and objective.
5. Draft the narrative before art-directing screenshots.
6. Produce media against the planned crop/state list.
7. Complete client, confidentiality, testimonial, and image-rights review.
8. Accessibility review all media, captions, video controls, heading order, and links.
9. Add structured `CreativeWork`/breadcrumb metadata only when it accurately describes the page.
10. Establish a six- or twelve-month evidence update rather than freezing launch claims forever.

## Success measures

- Prospects refer to a specific case or decision in enquiries.
- Case-to-contact and Work-to-case paths are measurable with privacy-appropriate analytics.
- Users can identify UI Forge's role and the project's outcome without reading the entire page.
- Technical readers find enough implementation evidence without overwhelming non-technical buyers.
- Every public claim has an internal source/permission record.
- Case studies remain legible and fully navigable on mobile, keyboard, reduced motion, and zoom.
